## 30-second elevator pitch

Organ-on-a-chip labs image their cultures every day, and those images become datasets and digital twins. A defocused or bubble-filled frame, or a language model that invents a citation, corrupts that asset silently. Zelos is a quality gate: physics tools compute the verdict, the cause and the lab action; open literature is cited; and a deterministic gate blocks every claim the evidence cannot support. It blocked 100/100 injected fabrications, it runs on a CPU in 168 ms, and it tells you where it fails.

## 12-minute script

Total: 12:00. Timings are start to end for each slide; spoken pace is about 140 words per minute.

### Slide 1: Title (0:00 to 0:30)

Hello, I am Philip, and this is Zelos: every organ-on-a-chip image, certified. In one sentence, Zelos looks at a brightfield image, tells you whether you can trust it, says what went wrong and what to do about it, cites open literature for the action, and refuses to say anything its evidence cannot back up. Over the next twelve minutes I will show you what works, what does not, and what I corrected since the submission.

### Slide 2: The problem (0:30 to 1:10)

Organ-on-a-chip labs image their cultures every day, and those images become datasets and, increasingly, the input to digital twins. Two things can go wrong, and both go wrong silently. A defocused, saturated or bubble-filled frame looks like any other file. And a language model asked to explain that frame can invent a number or a citation, and sound just as confident. Labs cannot review every frame by eye, so the question is whether the automatic check can itself be trusted.

### Slide 3: The certificate (1:10 to 2:10)

This is what Zelos outputs: a certificate. On the left is a real image where I injected a severity-5 over-exposure. Zelos fails it, routes it to re-imaging, names the cause, and gives the lab action. On the right is an untouched real image that passes. Each card lists the passage it cites and a ledger, where every sentence is linked to the tool output or the paper behind it. Look at the amber line on the left: for over-exposure our corpus does not support the action, best sentence 0.333, and the certificate says so rather than dressing it up.

### Slide 4: Architecture (2:10 to 2:55)

The design rule is the headline: the LLM proposes, tools compute, the gate disposes. 17 physics metrics and a fixed rule table compute the verdict, the cause and the route, with no language model at all. Cohere, Qdrant, BM25 and a reranker retrieve open-access papers. The language model only proposes sentences as typed claims, in at most 2 calls. Then a deterministic gate checks every number against a tool output and every citation against a retrieved passage, repairs once, and blocks what it cannot support. If the model is wrong or absent, the verdict is still correct.

### Slide 5: Result A, the trust layer (2:55 to 4:05)

First result: does the gate work? We injected 100 fabrications, 5 kinds, 20 each: fake numbers, fake citations, wrong support, uncited literature, orphan inferences. The gate caught 100/100, and it blocked 0/98 correct claims. On real model outputs, unsupported claims fall from 0.067 to 0.000, valid citations rise from 0.895 to 1.000, and unsafe adversarial outputs fall from 0.214 to 0.000. Because the gate does the checking, we could swap the model: 4 models ran behind it, and none of the 3 larger ones beat the small one. Now the caveat: the after-gate rate is zero by construction, and the support test is lexical, so it is a weak test of meaning.

### Slide 6: Result B, faults with known ground truth (4:05 to 5:15)

Second result, where we know the right answer because we injected the fault. 60 real images, 11 fault kinds, severities 1, 3 and 5, and a threshold calibrated to flag 3.3% of clean images. Zelos flags 63.3% of the severity 3 and 5 faults and names the right cause 85.4% of the time, where chance is 11.1%. Now the red bars, which are the honest part. At severity 3 Zelos found only debris 3.3%, uneven illumination 8.3% and motion blur 23.3%. Those are mostly missed. This benchmark is ours, so it measures sensitivity to these synthetic faults, not which faults matter most in a lab.

### Slide 7: Result C, head to head (5:15 to 6:25)

Third result: how does this compare with the obvious alternatives? I trained a linear head on frozen DINOv2 embeddings to predict the expert label, which is a common approach, and calibrated it to the same 10% false-alarm rule. On the same injected faults it finds 25.5% where Zelos finds 63.3%, and it cannot name a cause. To be fair to it: it was never trained on faults, it is my own implementation for comparison, and on the expert label it is stronger than our artifact score. Vision language models, given one prompt, find 90.0% to 100.0% of the severe faults but flag 40.0% to 65.0% of clean images. With n=20 per group and one prompt, treat that as a signal, not a verdict.

### Slide 8: Result D, the shift finding (6:25 to 7:20)

This finding changed the design. Fixed thresholds do not transfer between imaging sessions. When we leave a whole session out, thresholds calibrated on the others flag 21.3% and 17.6% of good images, against a 10% target. In the upper fifth of sessions, single-image scoring flags 28.4%. Scoring each image relative to its own session gives 9.7%. On the 39 test sessions the absolute modes happened to be conservative, 1.1% false flags, and found only 4.2% of the expert-bad images. So session-relative is the default, and single-image mode is the weaker option.

### Slide 9: The expert label (7:20 to 8:15)

Here is an honest slide. Against the expert good-or-bad label, the artifact score is at chance: 0.571, interval [0.493, 0.647]. A lookup against expert-labelled neighbours reaches 0.767, and within the same imaging day 0.735. So the expert label tracks the state of the culture, not the quality of the image. We do not claim state-of-the-art expert-label accuracy. Zelos reports the culture-state signal as a second output, and it answers a different question: can I trust this frame.

### Slide 10: Literature-backed actions (8:15 to 9:00)

Every lab action in the rule table is looked up in our 48-paper open corpus, sentence by sentence. 4 of the 9 are supported by a real sentence. 5 are not: sensor noise, over-exposure, under-exposure, debris, compression artefacts. The certificate says so instead of citing something loosely related. One disclosure: the gate's default threshold, 0.30, let off-topic sentences through, so I raised it to 0.50 after reading the matches. That is post hoc, and it is written in the results file.

### Slide 11: Reproducibility and engineering (9:00 to 9:30)

Everything is built to be re-run. CI runs on every push, every model response behind a published number is committed and replayed offline, and a provenance check fails the build if a README number is not in the results files. This deck is generated the same way. The tools are available as a CLI, an API, an MCP server and a skill file, there is a Docker image and an upload demo with strict limits, and a full inspection takes 168 milliseconds on a CPU.

### Slide 12: Limitations (9:30 to 10:15)

The list I would rather you hear from me. Single-image mode misses the false-alarm target on the upper fifth of unseen sessions. Cause naming is imperfect. 3 fault kinds are mostly missed. The expert label is not an image-fault label, and the culture-state lookup uses expert-labelled neighbours. It is one public dataset from one lab, with faults I injected. Several samples are small: 60 base images, 20 per vision-model group. Grounding checks are lexical. And 5 of 9 actions lack a literature basis. This is a research tool, not a medical device.

### Slide 13: Corrections to v1.0 (10:15 to 11:10)

Now the corrections. The submission, tag v1.0-submission, evaluated 480 of 3,072 images. Its pre-registered reference flagged 76.7% of clean test images; its artifact score had an AUROC of 0.393 on 301 images; and its cause attribution of 31.0% came from a different flagged set, so it is not comparable with the new figure. In v1.1 the scope is all 3,072 images, the artifact score is at chance with intervals that include chance level, so the conclusion stands but the number is superseded, and detection looks lower, 63.3% against 97.1%, because the operating point is now honest. v1.0 is what was submitted; v1.1 is a post-deadline build and I am labelling it as such.

### Slide 14: Roadmap (11:10 to 11:30)

Next steps: extend the certificate to the sponsor's neural organ-on-a-chip and electrode-array data, add per-well longitudinal monitoring, put an entailment model in the gate, rerun the rule citations on a bigger corpus when the API quota resets, and run a prospective validation with a partner lab.

### Slide 15: Closing (11:30 to 12:00)

To close: Zelos does not read the culture and does not diagnose anything. It certifies the frame, and every sentence it writes traces back to a tool or a paper. The live console is at zelos.philipsimonderock.com, the code is on GitHub, the submitted version is the tag v1.0-submission, and today's version is on the main branch. Thank you, and I am happy to take questions.
