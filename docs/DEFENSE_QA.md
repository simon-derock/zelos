# Zelos final-round defense: 25 hard questions

Generated by `scripts/build_defense_kit.py` from `results/*.json` (results produced at commit `7a1ea00` on 2026-10-10). Every figure below is read from the results files; nothing is typed by hand. Paths are relative to the repository root. Research tool, not a medical device.

Context for all answers: v1.0 (tag `v1.0-submission`) is what was submitted and evaluated. v1.1 (branch `next`) is a post-deadline build for the final round. "Held-out" always means whole imaging sessions that were not used to fit or calibrate anything.

---

### Q1. Why not just fine-tune a classifier on the expert labels?

We built that baseline and report it. A linear head on frozen DINOv2 embeddings, trained on the expert good/bad label, reaches AUROC 0.803 [0.760, 0.836] on 2,110 held-out images, which is higher than the Zelos artifact score (0.571 [0.493, 0.647]). The reason we do not stop there is what the head cannot do. On injected faults, at the same 10% session-averaged false-alarm rule, it finds 25.5% of severity 3 and 5 faults against 63.3% for Zelos session-relative, and it cannot name a cause or a lab action. It also needs expert labels for every new chip and microscope, while Zelos needs a reference batch. The two are complementary: the certificate carries a kNN culture-state output built from the same embeddings, with no model trained.

**Source:** `results/e2.json` → `detectors["LABEL-TRAINED dinov2 linear head (baseline)"]`; `results/e1.json` → `head_to_head.label_trained_dinov2_linear_head`.

### Q2. Why is your expert-label AUROC modest?

Because the Zelos artifact score is not built to predict the expert label, and it does not. It measures technical image faults, and on 2,110 held-out images from 39 sessions it sits at 0.571 [0.493, 0.647], with random at 0.487. The expert label tracks something about the culture: a lookup against expert-labelled neighbours reaches 0.767 [0.720, 0.816], and the label-trained head 0.803. We do not claim state-of-the-art expert-label accuracy. Scores also depend on the split: ours holds out whole imaging sessions and reports a within-day AUROC (0.735 for the kNN, 0.661 for the artifact score) to remove the imaging-day shortcut, so a number from a different split is not directly comparable.

**Source:** `results/e2.json` → `detectors[*].auroc`, `auroc_ci95`, `within_session.weighted`.

### Q3. What does the QC score mean biologically?

Nothing biological. The score is the maximum over metrics of a one-sided robust z-score divided by a calibrated per-metric cutoff; a value above 1 is flagged. It describes focus, exposure, contrast, illumination, noise, debris, bubbles and compression, that is, whether the frame is technically sound. It says nothing about viability, barrier integrity or whether a culture is "good". That is the reason the certificate has a second, separate output: a culture-state signal (a kNN lookup against expert-labelled images), which answers "does this culture resemble ones experts accepted?" and inherits whatever criteria those experts used, which we do not know.

**Source:** `results/calibration.json` → `score`, `rule`; `src/zelos/vision/culture.py`.

### Q4. Is the injected-fault benchmark circular? You wrote both the faults and the detector.

Partly, yes, and it is important to say what it does not show. It does not show that Zelos finds the faults that occur in real labs, how often they occur, or that our synthetic blur or bubble looks like real optics. What it does give is a known right answer: 60 real test-session images, 11 fault kinds, severities 1, 3 and 5, with cutoffs calibrated on expert-good images from separate calibration sessions and cause signatures fitted on 48 development images from other sessions. Its value is the comparison on identical inputs (Zelos vs the label-trained head vs vision LLMs) and the misses it exposes: debris 3.3% at severity 3; uneven illumination 8.3% at severity 3; motion blur 23.3% at severity 3. The absolute detection rate depends on a severity scale we chose, so read it as sensitivity to these synthetic faults only.

**Source:** `results/e1.json` → `variants.batch_session`, `per_kind`, `n_base_images`, `n_signature_images`.

### Q5. Why does the label-trained head do so badly on faults?

Be careful with the claim: this is one approach, implemented here for comparison. The head was trained to predict the expert culture label and never saw a fault. We calibrated it to the same rule as Zelos (smallest threshold whose mean per-session false-flag rate on out-of-fold expert-good development images is at most 10%), and at that point it flags 5.0% of clean images and detects 25.5% of severity 3 and 5 faults, AUROC 0.710. It does best on motion blur (60.8%) and worst on debris (1.7%), sensor noise (6.7%) and JPEG compression (10.8%). We checked the embedding path: the mean absolute probability difference between the two preprocessing paths on clean images is 0.010, and the clean flag rate on the extraction path is 5.0%. A head trained with fault augmentation could do better; we did not try that, so the comparison shows that expert-label models are not fault detectors, not that embeddings cannot be.

**Source:** `results/e1.json` → `head_to_head.label_trained_dinov2_linear_head` (`operating_point`, `detection_by_kind_sev3plus`, `mean_abs_prob_diff_between_paths`).

### Q6. How reliable is the grounding gate? Lexical support is a weak test.

Numbers and citation existence are checked deterministically, and the gate caught 100/100 injected fabrications of 5 kinds while blocking 0/98 correct claims. Literature support is lexical overlap, which is weak, and the rule-citation work shows it. The gate's default threshold of 0.30 accepted off-topic sentences for rule bases (best-sentence support 0.333 for over-exposure and 0.375 for debris would have passed at that level), so for rule bases we raised it to 0.50 after reading the matches. That choice is post hoc and disclosed in the results file. The unsupported rate after the gate is zero by construction, and the fabrication kinds are ones we wrote, so the honest reading is: reliable on numbers and fake citations, weak on meaning. An entailment model inside the gate is the main roadmap item.

**Source:** `results/e5.json` → `gate_fault_injection`; `results/rule_citations.json` → `theta_lex`, `disclosure`.

### Q7. Why do 5 of the 9 rules lack literature support?

Only 4 of 9 rules are supported (defocus or blur, uneven illumination, low contrast, air bubble); 5 are not (sensor noise, over-exposure, under-exposure, debris, compression artefacts). The corpus is 48 open-access organ-on-a-chip and imaging papers, and generic microscopy QC statements such as "saturated pixels lose information" are rarely written out in them. The match needs a single sentence that contains the required term groups and reaches lexical support of at least 0.50; the best under-exposure sentence scored 0.000. We mark these rules unsupported instead of citing a loosely related passage. That does not mean the action is wrong: the actions are written a priori as imaging practice guidance. A larger corpus is on the roadmap once the API quota resets.

**Source:** `results/rule_citations.json` → `rules[*].status`, `support`, `n_supported`.

### Q8. Why Cohere and Workers AI, and what happens offline?

Both have free tiers, both are swappable behind the gate, and neither is needed to reproduce a result. Every response used in the published numbers is recorded and replayed from `eval/llm_cache` and the retrieval cache, so `make judge` runs with no keys. Each service has an offline fallback: hashed vectors, BM25 order and a deterministic template explainer. The hosted Space uses the template explainer and makes no outbound calls. Swappability is measured: 4 models ran behind the same gate with zero unsupported claims after it. The Cohere chat model was not measured because the shared trial key hit its monthly quota (`docs/LLM_SWEEP.md`).

**Source:** `results/e5_sweep.json` → `models[*].status`; `docs/LLM_SWEEP.md`; `docs/SPACE.md`.

### Q10. What would you do with real longitudinal or neural organ-on-a-chip data?

First, replace the per-session reference with a per-well history, because Result D shows that the reference matters more than the threshold: session-relative scoring met the 10% false-alarm target (9.7% leave-one-session-out) where absolute cutoffs did not (21.3% and 17.6%). Second, score trajectories and drift, not only single frames. Third, for neural or electrode-array data the metrics change, but the structure stays: tools compute a verdict and cause, a rule table maps to a lab action, retrieval cites open literature, the gate blocks unsupported text. We would calibrate with leave-one-session-out exactly as in `results/calibration.json`. None of this has been evaluated: we have only brightfield data.

**Source:** `results/calibration.json` → `variants[*].loso_mean_false_flag_rate`; roadmap in `docs/WRITEUP.md` section 8.

### Q11. What is the regulatory scope?

Research use only. Zelos is not a medical device and makes no clinical or diagnostic claims; the router refuses diagnosis requests and prompt injections before any model call, and in the adversarial set the gated unsafe-output rate is 0.000 (n=14) against 0.214 ungated. It is not validated for regulated (GxP) use: certificates are SHA-256 content hashes of the image and ledger, not cryptographic signatures, and there is no electronic-record audit trail. A regulated deployment would need a validated reference set from the lab, signatures, change control and a prospective study.

**Source:** `results/e5.json` → `conditions.llm_gated.adversarial`; `docs/LIMITATIONS.md`.

### Q12. What changed between v1.0 and v1.1, and why is that legitimate?

The v1.0 build is frozen at tag `v1.0-submission` and nothing in it was rewritten. The v1.1 build is separate on branch `next`, labelled as post-deadline, with a full changelog in `docs/V1_1_CHANGES.md`. It is legitimate for four reasons. The changes respond to failures that v1.0 itself reported (cross-session shift, the pre-registered reference). Every v1.1 number is on whole held-out sessions with session-cluster confidence intervals. Post-hoc choices are disclosed in the results files. And the headline number fell for a good reason: detection went from 97.1% to 63.3% because v1.0's operating point flagged 76.7% of clean images, while v1.1's flags 3.3%.

**Source:** `docs/V1_1_CHANGES.md`; `results/e1.json`; git tag `v1.0-submission`.

### Q13. Why did v1.0 use 480 images when 3,072 exist, and what changed?

The v1.0 build used a seeded, stratified subset of 480 images fetched by CRC-checked byte-range requests, so the full archive was never downloaded; that kept the submission reproducible on a laptop but evaluated 480 of 3,072 images. The v1.1 build streams all 3,072 once, keeps only features (physics metrics and a frozen DINOv2-small embedding) and discards the pixels. The expert-label evaluation now has 2,110 test images from 39 sessions, of which 968 are expert-bad. The injected-fault benchmark still uses 60 base images because injection needs pixels that are no longer kept.

**Source:** `results/e2.json` → `n_images`, `n_sessions`, `n_test`; `scripts/extract_features.py`.

### Q14. Why hold out whole sessions?

Frames from one imaging session share acquisition conditions and often a culture batch, so a random image split would let a model learn the session instead of the culture or the fault. Result D shows how large the session effect is: absolute cutoffs calibrated on some sessions flagged 21.3% and 17.6% of good images from unseen ones, against a 10% target. We therefore split by session, report session-cluster bootstrap intervals, and add a within-day AUROC that cannot be explained by the imaging date.

**Source:** `results/calibration.json` → `loso_mean_false_flag_rate`; `results/e2.json` → `within_session`.

### Q15. What does the vision-LLM baseline show, and what does it not show?

It shows that general vision LLMs, with one prompt and no tuning, flag a large share of clean images (40.0% to 65.0%) and of expert-good images (45.0% to 65.0%), while finding 90.0% to 100.0% of severity-5 faults against 80.0% for Zelos. As a gate that false-alarm rate is unusable. It does not show what a better prompt, few-shot examples or a stronger model would do: there is one prompt, 2 models with usable replies, and n=20 images per group, so every rate carries a wide interval. One model returned no parsable reply on 80 cases and is reported as such.

**Source:** `results/vlm_baseline.json` → `models`, `design`, `provenance.prompt`, `zelos_stored_reference_calibrated`.

### Q16. What do cost and latency look like?

The physics QC runs on a CPU: the full inspection has a p50 of 168 ms (p95 202 ms) and the metric suite alone 379 ms, measured on 480 images in the v1.0 run, so this figure was not re-measured on all images. The gated LLM explanation costs 1,280 tokens and 0.87 calls per answer with a p50 of 1,886 ms. In the sweep (n=20 cases), Llama 3.1 8B costs $0.0182 per 100 answers and the most expensive model $0.2069. The DINOv2 embedding behind the culture-state output is an optional extra step: it needs torch, which the light Space image leaves out.

**Source:** `results/e9.json`; `results/e5.json` → `conditions.llm_gated`; `results/e5_sweep.json` → `groups.explain_qc.cost`.

### Q17. Is the upload demo secure?

It is hardened, not audited. The server accepts PNG, JPEG or TIFF up to 25 MB and 40 megapixels, checks the header before decoding as a decompression-bomb guard, processes images in memory and never writes or logs them, rate-limits to 20 requests per minute per client with a global ceiling and a concurrency cap, and allows only listed CORS origins. The explainer is the deterministic template, so the Space holds no API keys and makes no outbound calls. Limits to state plainly: there is no authentication, it runs on a free CPU tier that sleeps when idle, there has been no external penetration test, and it should not receive confidential images.

**Source:** `docs/SPACE.md`; `src/zelos/api/main.py`; `tests/test_api_hardening.py`.

### Q18. How would this integrate into a lab imaging pipeline?

At three points. After an acquisition run, `zelos triage <folder>` scores the session against its own batch reference (the API needs a batch of 4 to 12 images for that) and returns a re-image list. In an acquisition script, `POST /api/inspect` returns a certificate with a route (`RE-IMAGE`, `INSPECT-CHIP`, `RE-EXPORT` or `USE`) while the plate is still on the stage. At deposit, the certificate JSON (image SHA-256, metrics, verdict, cause, action, evidence, claim ledger) is stored next to the image in the data catalogue. Any MCP-capable agent can call the same tools through the MCP server and `skills/zelos-qc/SKILL.md`. Single-image mode exists but is the weaker mode.

**Source:** `src/zelos/cli.py`; `src/zelos/api/main.py`; `skills/zelos-qc/SKILL.md`; `configs/routes.yaml`.

### Q19. How does this align with the sponsor's interest in data assets and digital twins?

Zelos is a certification gate in front of the asset. An organ-on-a-chip digital twin is only as good as the images and measurements that feed it, and a bad frame or an invented citation enters silently. Each certificate is a machine-readable record that says what was measured, what was concluded, which passage supports it, and what was blocked. We evaluated brightfield images only. The sponsor's neural organ-on-a-chip and electrode-array data is the first roadmap item, and we make no claim that the current metrics transfer to it.

**Source:** `docs/WRITEUP.md` sections 2 and 8; certificate schema in `src/zelos/agent/`.

### Q20. Is the retrieval result too good to be true?

Read it as a sanity check, not a headline. Hybrid retrieval with reranking reaches paper-level MRR 1.000 on 61 queries over 48 papers and declines 12/12 unanswerable questions, but the gold questions were written from the corpus text itself and spot-checked, so the task is easy and there is no headroom. The chunk-level Hit@1 of 0.852 is the more discriminating number. Mode differences (BM25 0.944, dense 0.973, hybrid 0.951) are within noise. This result says retrieval works on a clean, small corpus; it does not say it will find the right paper for an arbitrary lab question.

**Source:** `results/e4.json` → `modes`, `abstention`, `gold`; `eval/README_retrieval.md`.

### Q21. Cause attribution: where does it fail?

Session-relative signatures name the injected cause for 85.4% of flagged faults (rule table 72.6%, top-2 90.6%, chance 11.1%). It is strong for noise (95.6%), bubbles (94.4%) and compression (95.6%). It is weak for uneven illumination (54.5%) and debris (45.5%), which are also mostly missed. In the confusion matrix, blur and under-exposure are often read as low contrast, and over-exposure sometimes as a bubble. Signatures are fitted on our own injected faults, so this is optimistic for real data. The certificate always shows the top-2 list and the metric values, so a technician can overrule the primary label.

**Source:** `results/e1.json` → `variants.batch_session.per_kind`, `confusion_v2_sev3plus`; `results/signatures.json`.

### Q22. Will the thresholds transfer to a new microscope or chip?

We do not know. Everything here is one public dataset from one lab and one automated microscope. The session-relative reference adapts to a new session's illumination and exposure, but the per-metric cutoffs were calibrated on expert-good images from this dataset. The procedure transfers, not the numbers: take a partner's expert-good frames from several sessions, fit cutoffs so that the leave-one-session-out false-flag rate stays at or below 10%, and report the session spread as in `results/calibration.json` (median 3.7%, 80th percentile 13.3% for session-relative). Until that is done on another instrument, any threshold claim is limited to this data.

**Source:** `results/calibration.json` → `rule`, `loso_session_rates_p50_p80`; `docs/LIMITATIONS.md`.

### Q23. Does the culture-state kNN leak labels?

Not in the evaluation, but there are two caveats. In the evaluation the bank holds only fit and calibration sessions, and test sessions are never in it, so the 0.767 [0.720, 0.816] is a held-out figure; the within-day value is 0.735 and the session-centred variant 0.692. In the product, the bank holds all 3,072 labelled images, so a new image from a session already in the bank will look better than one from a new session. And the neighbours carry the expert's judgement, whose criteria we do not know and which may partly reflect cell line, time after seeding or density. "Culture state" is our reading of the signal, not something we verified biologically.

**Source:** `results/e2.json` → `detectors["knn_dinov2_cls_k15"]`, `detectors["knn_dinov2_session_centred_k15"]`; `src/zelos/vision/culture.py`.

### Q24. Is the LLM worth having at all?

For the verdict, no: the verdict, cause and action are computed without it, and the template explanation is fully grounded. The measured case for the LLM is narrower. Behind the gate it produces more supported claims per case than the template (6.15 against 4.90 for QC explanations, 2.67 against 0.89 for literature questions), but claim count rewards verbosity and not usefulness. It costs 0.87 calls and 1,886 ms per answer. The sweep also shows that a bigger or dearer model does not help (Llama 3.1 8B leads with 6.15 claims per case). If you do not need prose, you can switch it off and lose no safety.

**Source:** `results/e5.json` → `verdict`; `results/e5_sweep.json` → `verdict`.

### Q25. Which choices were post hoc, and what would falsify the claims?

Disclosed post-hoc choices: the batch reference was added after v1.0's cross-session result; the signature-centroid attribution and the per-metric calibration were designed after v1.0's failures; the rule-citation threshold was raised from 0.30 to 0.50 after reading the matches; reasoning-model settings in the sweep were chosen by one probe each. No threshold was tuned on test sessions in v1.1, but we had seen v1.0's test results before designing v1.1, so a garden-of-forking-paths concern is real, and only a prospective study can remove it. The claims would be falsified by a partner lab's frames on which session-relative cutoffs miss the 10% false-alarm target by a wide margin, or by an entailment model that rejects many gate-approved literature claims.

**Source:** `results/rule_citations.json` → `disclosure`; `results/e1.json` → `variants`; `docs/V1_1_CHANGES.md`.
